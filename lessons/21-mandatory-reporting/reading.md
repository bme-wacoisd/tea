# When You Must Speak Up

A first-year teacher notices a student wearing long sleeves in September heat. The same student, usually chatty, has gone quiet. During recess, the teacher sees circular marks on the child's forearm when they reach for a ball. The teacher's stomach drops. Those look like cigarette burns. But the parent volunteers at school events. The parent seems loving. Maybe the child fell onto something? Maybe it's a skin condition? The teacher mentions it to a colleague, who says, "Yeah, I noticed that too. Somebody should probably say something." Two weeks later, that child arrives at school with a broken wrist. The investigation reveals months of escalating abuse. Multiple adults had noticed. None had reported. Texas law says you must report within 48 hours of forming a reasonable suspicion. Not suspicion plus certainty. Not suspicion plus corroboration. Just suspicion. The law exists because hesitation allows harm to continue, and children don't have 48 days to wait while adults gather the courage to act.

Here's what the law actually requires: If you suspect a child is being abused or neglected, you personally must call the Texas Abuse Hotline at 1-800-252-5400 within 48 hours. Not "tell the principal and assume they'll handle it." Not "talk to the counselor and see what they think." You. The law makes you a mandatory reporter because you're uniquely positioned to notice. You see the same children every day. You notice when a cheerful kid goes silent. You notice when a child flinches at raised voices. You notice when a student wolfs down every scrap of breakfast like they're starving. You notice the signs that a parent only sees for an hour after work or a doctor sees once a year. The hesitation is understandable—what if you're wrong? What if you destroy a family over a misunderstanding? But here's the thing: your job is not to be certain. Your job is to notice and report. Trained investigators will determine what's actually happening. You're not testifying in court. You're making a phone call that says, "I saw something that worried me." That's it. And the law protects you for doing exactly that.

The hardest part isn't knowing the hotline number. It's overcoming the voice in your head that says, "Maybe I'm overreacting." You'll tell yourself the parent seems nice—but abusers don't wear signs. They coach soccer. They bring cupcakes to class parties. They're charming, successful, well-liked. That's often how they avoid detection. You'll tell yourself you don't have proof—but the standard isn't proof, it's reasonable suspicion. If you saw something that made you uneasy, that's enough. You'll tell yourself it's not your place—but Texas law literally says it is your place. You're a mandatory reporter. You'll tell yourself someone else must have noticed too—maybe, but they might be having this exact same internal conversation, waiting for you to act. Here's what happens after you report: Child Protective Services investigates. Sometimes they determine the concern was unfounded—maybe those bruises really were from soccer, and the family gets a clean bill of health. Sometimes they find a struggling family that needs support—food assistance, parenting classes, counseling—and the family gets help instead of punishment. Sometimes they find serious danger, remove the child, and save a life. You'll never know which outcome your call leads to because CPS doesn't report back to you. But you'll know you did your job. You noticed. You spoke up. You gave that child a chance.

If a child discloses abuse to you directly, the pressure triples. They've chosen you, out of all the adults in their world, as the one safe enough to tell. Stay calm. Your reaction will shape whether they ever tell another adult anything difficult again. Don't gasp. Don't cry. Don't say, "Are you sure?" or "That can't be true." Just listen. When they finish, say, "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. You were brave to tell me, and I'm going to make sure you get help." Then call the hotline and repeat what the child said in their words, not your interpretation. You might feel like you're betraying the child's trust by reporting—they might have even asked you to keep it secret—but keeping that secret means keeping them in danger. The adults who hurt children often tell them, "Don't tell anyone, or bad things will happen." Your job is to show the child that telling a trusted adult makes bad things stop, not start.

The stakes are real. Failing to report is a Class A misdemeanor in Texas. You can lose your teaching certificate. You can face jail time. But the legal consequences are not why you'll report. You'll report because a seven-year-old shouldn't have to figure out how to survive violence on their own. You'll report because you became a teacher to help kids, and sometimes helping looks like making a hard phone call. You'll report because someday, maybe years later, you'll run into a former student who's thriving, and you'll remember the day you noticed they weren't okay and did something about it. Save the hotline number in your phone right now: 1-800-252-5400. Screenshot it. Write it on a sticky note. You might never need it. But if you do, you won't have time to google it while a child is standing in front of you waiting to see if you're the adult who finally acts.
